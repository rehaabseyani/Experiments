# Rebalancing

## What It Is

Rebalancing is the process of **restoring your portfolio to its original target [[asset-allocation]]** after market movements have pushed it out of balance.

## Why Portfolios Drift

Markets don't move uniformly. If stocks have a great year and bonds don't, a 60/40 stock/bond portfolio might become 80/20 — making you significantly more exposed to equity risk than you intended, without any decision on your part.

**Example (from investor.gov):**
> You start with 60% stocks / 40% bonds. After a bull market, stocks rise to 80% of your portfolio. To rebalance, you sell some stocks and/or buy more bonds to return to 60/40.

## Why It Works: Buy Low, Sell High

Rebalancing *automatically* enforces a buy-low, sell-high discipline:
- You sell the asset class that has *outperformed* (selling high)
- You buy the asset class that has *underperformed* (buying low)

This is counterintuitive — moving money away from a winner feels wrong — but it is a rational, systematic approach to maintaining your intended risk level.

## When to Rebalance

Two common strategies:

| Strategy | Trigger |
|---|---|
| **Calendar-based** | Rebalance every 6 or 12 months |
| **Threshold-based** | Rebalance when any asset class drifts more than a set % (e.g., 5%) from target |

Both work. Most experts agree rebalancing works best when done **infrequently** — over-trading increases costs and taxes.

## Automatic Rebalancing

**Target date funds** handle rebalancing automatically. The fund's manager shifts allocation over time — becoming more conservative as you approach the target year — so you don't have to. This is a good option for investors who don't want to manage rebalancing manually.

## Tax Considerations

In taxable accounts, selling appreciated assets to rebalance may trigger capital gains taxes. Strategies to mitigate this:
- Rebalance inside tax-advantaged accounts (401(k), IRA) first
- Direct new contributions toward underweight asset classes instead of selling
