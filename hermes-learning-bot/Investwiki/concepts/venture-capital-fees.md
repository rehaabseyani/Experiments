# Venture Capital Fees

Venture capital (VC) funds charge investors two types of fees: a **management fee** and **carried interest** (also called "carry"). Together they are commonly referred to as the **"2 and 20"** structure — 2% management fee plus 20% of profits.

Understanding these fees is essential because they directly reduce your net returns.

## The Two Fee Types at a Glance

| Fee | Typical Rate | What It Pays For |
|-----|-------------|-----------------|
| Management fee | ~2% per year | Fund operating costs (salaries, offices, legal) |
| Carried interest | ~20% of profits | GP's share of investment profits |

## Management Fee (the "2")

The management fee is an **annual charge** calculated as a percentage of committed capital — the total amount investors have pledged to the fund, not just what has been deployed into startups yet.

**Example:** You commit $100,000 to a 10-year VC fund with a 2% annual management fee.

- Annual fee: $100,000 × 2% = **$2,000/year**
- Total management fees over 10 years: **$20,000**
- Capital actually invested in startups: **$80,000** (not $100,000)

This is a critical point: **management fees reduce the amount of your money that gets put to work.** Even before the fund makes a single investment, a portion of your commitment is being used to pay the fund's operating expenses.

For a large fund, 2% of $100 million = $2 million per year — enough to pay salaries, rent offices, and travel to meet founders. For a small $10 million fund, that's only $200,000/year, which is why small-fund managers depend heavily on carry to make a living.

### Step-Downs

Many VC funds reduce management fees after the active investment period (years 1–5) ends. Once the fund stops making new investments and is simply managing existing ones, fees may step down to 1.5% or even 1% of invested capital (the amount actually deployed, which shrinks as companies exit). This prevents the fund from collecting high fees while passively waiting for exits.

## Carried Interest / "Carry" (the "20")

Carried interest is the GP's share of the fund's **profits** — the real upside they earn when investments succeed. Carry is typically **20% of profits**, but only after investors first get back all the capital they invested.

**Example:** A fund raises $10 million, invests it, and eventually returns $30 million.

1. First, the $10 million invested capital is returned to LPs — GPs get nothing yet
2. The remaining $20 million is profit; it gets split 80/20
3. LPs receive $16 million (80%), GPs receive $4 million carry (20%)

From the LP perspective: they committed $10 million + paid $2 million in management fees over 10 years ($12 million true cost) and received $26 million back. The fund returned 3x gross, but net returns after fees are about **2.2x**.

**Why carry exists:** Carry is an incentive mechanism. GPs only earn significant income when the fund performs well. If a fund just returns LP capital (1x) with no profit, carry is zero. This aligns the GP's financial interests with investors' interests — both want exits at high valuations.

### Hurdle Rate

Some VC funds include a **hurdle rate** (also called a preferred return) — a minimum annual return LPs must receive before carry kicks in. A common hurdle is 8% per year. If the fund doesn't beat this threshold, the GP earns no carry at all.

### High-Water Mark

A **high-water mark** prevents GPs from collecting carry on recoveries after losses. If the fund declines and then recovers, the GP must first make investors whole before charging carry again.

## How "2 and 20" Affects Your Net Returns

The compounding effect of fees on VC returns is significant:

| Gross Fund Return | Management Fees (10yr, 2%) | Carry (20% of profits) | LP Net Return |
|------------------|--------------------------|----------------------|--------------|
| 2x ($100M → $200M) | ~$20M | $20M | ~1.6x |
| 3x ($100M → $300M) | ~$20M | $40M | ~2.2x |
| 5x ($100M → $500M) | ~$20M | $76M | ~3.6x |

*(Approximate; actual waterfall mechanics vary by fund.)*

Even in successful funds, fees and carry take a meaningful slice. This is one reason index funds are often recommended for most investors — their expense ratios are typically 0.03–0.20%, compared to 2% annual fees plus 20% profit-share in VC.

## Fees in Other Private Funds

Similar fee structures appear in related fund types (as noted by the SEC's Investor.gov):

- **Hedge funds:** typically 1–2% management fee + 15–20% performance fee
- **Private equity:** similar to VC, often with higher minimum investments

## What Questions to Ask Before Investing

If you ever become eligible to invest in a VC fund, ask:

1. What is the management fee rate, and does it step down after the investment period?
2. What is the carry rate, and is there a hurdle rate?
3. How do fees and expenses compare to other funds with similar strategies?
4. What is the fund's track record net of fees (not gross)?

## Key Takeaways for Beginners

- **"2 and 20"** is shorthand for the standard VC fee structure: 2% annual management fee + 20% of profits
- **Management fees** come out regardless of performance and reduce how much of your money is actually invested
- **Carry** rewards the GP when the fund succeeds, aligning their interests with yours — but takes a significant share of gains
- **Net returns always matter more than gross** — always ask for returns after fees
- VC is generally only accessible to accredited investors; most beginners are better served by low-cost [[index-funds]]
