# Diversification

## What It Is

Diversification is the practice of spreading investments across many different assets so that poor performance in one area is offset by better performance in others. The classic formulation: **"Don't put all your eggs in one basket."**

It is the *how* that implements [[asset-allocation]] — allocation decides the proportions; diversification makes sure each slice is itself spread out.

## Two Levels of Diversification

### 1. Across Asset Classes
Hold stocks *and* bonds *and* cash. Market conditions that hurt stocks (e.g., rising interest rates) often benefit bonds, and vice versa. See [[asset-classes]].

### 2. Within Asset Classes
Even within stocks, don't hold only one company or one sector. Spread across:
- Multiple companies
- Multiple industry sectors (technology, healthcare, consumer goods, energy…)
- Multiple geographies (domestic + international)

## Diversification via Funds

Mutual funds and ETFs make it easy to diversify instantly — each fund holds a basket of securities, so buying one fund can give you exposure to dozens or hundreds of companies.

**Caveat:** a narrowly-focused fund (e.g., a single-sector ETF) does *not* provide broad diversification on its own. If you hold multiple funds, check their top holdings — overlapping holdings reduce the benefit. See [[asset-classes]] for more on funds.

## What Diversification Does (and Doesn't) Do

| Does | Doesn't |
|---|---|
| Reduce company-specific (unsystematic) risk | Eliminate market-wide (systematic) risk |
| Smooth out volatility over time | Guarantee a profit |
| Protect against any single investment blowing up | Protect against a general market crash |

## Relationship to Other Concepts
