# Risk Tolerance

## What It Is

Risk tolerance is your **ability and willingness to lose some or all of your original investment** in exchange for potentially greater returns. It is one of the two personal inputs — alongside [[time-horizon]] — that shape your [[asset-allocation]].

## Two Dimensions

| Dimension | Description |
|---|---|
| **Ability** | Can you financially survive a significant loss? Do you have an emergency fund? Is the money needed for a near-term goal? |
| **Willingness** | Can you psychologically stay the course when markets drop? Will you panic-sell? |

Both matter. An investor with high ability but low willingness often makes poor decisions at market bottoms.

## The Risk–Return Tradeoff

Higher potential returns come with higher risk. Stocks have historically outperformed bonds over long periods, but with far more volatility. There is no "high return, low risk" option in efficient markets — that trade-off is a fundamental law of investing.

- **High risk tolerance** → comfortable with equity-heavy allocation; willing to accept large short-term swings for long-term gains
- **Low risk tolerance** → prefers capital preservation; may sacrifice upside for stability

## How to Assess Yours

- Online questionnaires: many investment websites offer these for free. Note that results can be biased toward the sponsor's own products.
- Ask: "If my portfolio dropped 30% in a month, would I sell, hold, or buy more?" Your honest answer reveals a lot.
- Your [[time-horizon]] also adjusts effective risk tolerance — a longer runway makes temporary losses less damaging.

## Relationship to Other Concepts
- [[asset-allocation]] — risk tolerance directly shapes your target allocation
- [[time-horizon]] — longer horizons can accommodate higher risk
- [[diversification]] — the primary practical tool for managing risk
- [[rebalancing]] — keeps actual risk in line with intended risk
